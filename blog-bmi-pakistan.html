BMI Categories: Standard vs South Asian Guidelines

The standard World Health Organization BMI categories were developed using data primarily from Western populations. Research has since shown that South Asians — including Pakistanis, Indians, and Bangladeshis — face higher health risks at lower BMI levels. This led to the development of revised cutoff points specifically for Asian populations.

Underweight

Below 18.5

Standard WHO cutoff. Same for South Asians.

Normal Weight

18.5 — 22.9

Revised lower cutoff for South Asians vs 24.9 standard.

Overweight

23.0 — 27.4

South Asian cutoff starts at 23, not 25.

Obese

27.5 and above

South Asian obese cutoff is 27.5 vs 30 standard.

📊 According to the National Library of Medicine, South Asians have significantly higher body fat percentage at the same BMI compared to Caucasians, and face increased risk of type 2 diabetes and cardiovascular disease at BMI levels as low as 23. Pakistan's health guidelines now reflect these revised cutoffs.

Pakistan's Weight Crisis: The Numbers

Pakistan is facing a serious and growing obesity problem. According to the World Health Organization Pakistan country profile, approximately 27% of Pakistani adults are overweight and 5% are obese — numbers that have grown significantly in the past two decades as Pakistan has urbanized and dietary habits have shifted toward processed, high-calorie foods.

The situation is particularly concerning because obesity in Pakistan disproportionately affects urban women. Research published in the Journal of Pakistan Medical Association found that urban Pakistani women have significantly higher rates of overweight and obesity compared to men and rural populations — driven by sedentary lifestyles, cultural norms around physical activity for women, and changes in diet.

Meanwhile, undernutrition remains a significant problem in rural Pakistan. This "double burden" — obesity in cities and undernutrition in rural areas — makes understanding your personal BMI even more important, as both extremes carry serious health risks.

What Health Conditions Are Linked to High BMI in Pakistan?

Being overweight or obese increases the risk of several conditions that are already among the leading causes of death and disability in Pakistan:

Type 2 Diabetes

Pakistan has one of the highest diabetes rates in the world. According to the International Diabetes Federation, Pakistan has approximately 33 million people living with diabetes — the third highest number of any country globally. Excess weight, particularly around the abdomen, is the single largest modifiable risk factor for type 2 diabetes. Because South Asians develop insulin resistance at lower BMI levels, the risk for Pakistanis begins even before they reach the standard "overweight" category.

Heart Disease and High Blood Pressure

Cardiovascular disease is the leading cause of death in Pakistan. High BMI increases blood pressure, raises LDL cholesterol, and promotes inflammation — all major risk factors for heart attacks and strokes. The risk is compounded by common Pakistani dietary habits: high salt intake from traditional cooking, ghee-heavy foods, and low physical activity levels in urban settings.

Joint Problems

Every extra kilogram of body weight puts approximately 4 kilograms of additional pressure on the knee joints. For Pakistani adults who are 10 to 20 kilograms overweight, this means 40 to 80 kilograms of extra knee stress with every step — explaining why knee pain and osteoarthritis are so common in middle-aged Pakistani adults.

Sleep Apnea

Obesity is the primary risk factor for obstructive sleep apnea — a condition where breathing repeatedly stops during sleep. This leads to poor sleep quality, daytime fatigue, and increased risk of heart disease. It is significantly underdiagnosed in Pakistan but extremely common in overweight adults.

BMI for Pakistani Children and Teenagers

BMI for children and teenagers (ages 2 to 18) is calculated the same way as adults but interpreted differently. Instead of fixed categories, children's BMI is compared to growth charts based on age and gender — called BMI-for-age percentiles.

  • Below 5th percentile: Underweight
  • 5th to 84th percentile: Healthy weight
  • 85th to 94th percentile: Overweight
  • 95th percentile and above: Obese

Childhood obesity in Pakistan has been increasing rapidly, driven by increased screen time, reduced outdoor play, and greater consumption of packaged snacks and sugary drinks. [Source: UNICEF Pakistan — Nutrition] Establishing healthy weight habits in childhood is far easier than reversing obesity in adulthood.

BMI and Ramadan: What Pakistani Muslims Should Know

For Pakistan's Muslim majority, Ramadan brings significant changes in eating patterns that can affect weight and BMI. Many Pakistanis actually gain weight during Ramadan despite fasting — because iftar and sehri meals are often calorie-dense, fried, and high in sugar.

Research shows that for people with high BMI, Ramadan fasting done correctly — with balanced, nutrient-rich iftar and sehri meals, adequate hydration, and light exercise — can actually lead to modest weight loss and improved blood sugar control. However, breaking the fast with heavy fried foods and sugary drinks often results in weight gain instead.

Practical Steps to Improve Your BMI

If your BMI is higher than the healthy range for Pakistani adults, these evidence-based approaches are the most effective:

Walk 30 minutes daily

Walking is the most accessible exercise for most Pakistanis — no gym, no equipment, no cost. A daily 30-minute walk at a moderate pace burns approximately 150 calories and improves insulin sensitivity, heart health, and mood. After Fajr prayer is an ideal time — the air is fresh, temperatures are cool, and it starts the day with physical activity.

Replace white rice and bread with whole grains

Traditional Pakistani diets are heavy in refined carbohydrates — white rice, maida roti, and white bread. Switching to brown rice, whole wheat atta, and adding more daal and sabzi to meals reduces calorie density and improves blood sugar control without requiring major dietary changes.

Reduce oil in cooking

Pakistani cooking traditionally uses generous amounts of oil and ghee. Reducing cooking oil by half — from 4 tablespoons to 2 tablespoons per dish — saves approximately 250 calories per meal with minimal impact on taste. Over one year, this single change can result in significant weight loss.

Drink water instead of chai and cold drinks

The average Pakistani adult drinks 3 to 5 cups of chai per day, each containing 50 to 100 calories from milk and sugar. Replacing half of these with water eliminates 75 to 250 calories daily — potentially 5 to 9 kilograms per year without any other changes.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a healthy BMI for a Pakistani woman?
For Pakistani women, a BMI between 18.5 and 22.9 is considered healthy using South Asian guidelines. BMI 23 to 27.4 indicates overweight, and 27.5 or above indicates obesity. These cutoffs are lower than Western standards because South Asians carry more body fat at the same BMI.
Is BMI accurate for muscular people?
BMI does not distinguish between fat and muscle mass. A person with high muscle mass can have a high BMI without being unhealthy. For most people however, BMI provides a useful general screening tool. Athletes should use additional measures like waist circumference alongside BMI.
How do I convert my height from feet to centimeters?
Multiply your height in feet by 30.48. For example, 5 feet 6 inches = 5.5 x 30.48 = 167.6 cm. Enter 168 in our BMI Calculator. Our Percentage Calculator can also help with unit conversion calculations.
Should I see a doctor if my BMI is high?
A high BMI is a signal worth discussing with a doctor — especially if you have other risk factors like family history of diabetes, high blood pressure, or joint pain. Your doctor can recommend appropriate tests and a safe, personalized plan. BMI alone is not a diagnosis.
