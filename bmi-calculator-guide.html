What Is BMI and Why Does It Matter?

BMI is a simple ratio of your weight to your height. It doesn't measure body fat directly, but it gives a quick, standardized estimate that's useful for spotting general trends — like whether someone falls into an underweight, healthy, overweight, or obese range. Because it only needs two numbers, it's become the global standard for a first-pass health screening.

How BMI Is Calculated

The standard formula divides weight in kilograms by height in meters squared. For people using pounds and inches, the formula adjusts with a different constant. The math itself isn't complicated, but converting units correctly is where most manual calculations go wrong — especially when switching between metric and imperial systems.

Understanding Your BMI Result

Once you get your score, it generally falls into one of four broad categories:

  • Below 18.5 — Underweight
  • 18.5 to 24.9 — Healthy weight range
  • 25 to 29.9 — Overweight
  • 30 and above — Obese

These ranges are useful for general awareness, but they're not a diagnosis. Muscle mass, age, bone density, and body composition all affect how meaningful a single BMI number really is for any one person.

Limitations of BMI

BMI is a screening tool, not a complete health assessment. A muscular athlete can register as "overweight" on a BMI scale despite having very low body fat, while someone with low muscle mass might fall into the "healthy" range despite carrying excess fat. That's why doctors usually combine BMI with other measurements — like waist circumference or body fat percentage — before drawing any conclusions.

Final Thoughts
